2 المفاهيم الكيميائية الأساسية والمصادر Chemical Concepts, Definitions & Sources

Q3 Short Written Explain the etymological meaning of "Carbohydrate", distinguish between Hydrated Carbons and Hydrocarbons, and state dietary sources.

Model Answer:
  • Etymological Meaning: The word "Carbohydrate" means Hydrated Carbon (Carbon + Water / C + H2O).
  • Distinction between Hydrated Carbons and Hydrocarbons:
    • Hydrated Carbons (Carbohydrates): Each carbon atom is associated with a water molecule (C + H2O), making them water-soluble (hydrophilic). Example: Table sugar dissolves easily in tea or water.
    • Hydrocarbons: Composed strictly of long carbon and hydrogen chains (C + H), lacking water molecules, making them water-insoluble (hydrophobic). Example: Fatty acids and lipids.
  • Dietary Proportion: Carbohydrates constitute approximately 60% of the daily human diet.
  • Dietary Sources:
    • Plant Sources: Bread, Rice, Pasta, Potatoes → Starch (complex poly-glucose).
    • Animal Sources: Milk → Lactose (milk sugar).
    • Animal Tissue: Liver and Muscle → Glycogen (complex animal starch stored in liver and muscle tissue).

Q5 Scientific Definition & Derivation Define Carbohydrates scientifically, define a Polyhydric Alcohol, and explain how the smallest functional carbohydrates are derived from Glycerol.

Model Answer:
  • Scientific Definition: Carbohydrates are Aldehyde or Ketone derivatives of Polyhydric Alcohols, or substances that yield them upon hydrolysis.
  • Polyhydric Alcohol Definition: An alcohol containing 3 or more hydroxyl (−OH) groups. (Monohydric = 1 −OH, Dihydric = 2 −OH).
  • Smallest Polyhydric Alcohol: Glycerol, which contains 3 carbon atoms and 3 hydroxyl groups (3 C + 3 −OH).
  • Derivation of Smallest Carbohydrates (Trioses):
    1. Oxidation of the primary alcohol group of Glycerol yields Glyceraldehyde (the smallest aldotriose carbohydrate).
    2. Oxidation of the secondary alcohol group of Glycerol yields Dihydroxyacetone (the smallest ketotriose carbohydrate).
    3. Phosphorylated forms: Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate and Dihydroxyacetone-phosphate are also valid carbohydrates derived from glycerol.
  • Sorbitol: A 6-carbon polyhydric alcohol containing 6 −OH groups; its oxidation yields Glucose or Fructose.

3 التصنيف العام والتغذية General Classification & Nutrition

Q7 Short Written Classify Carbohydrates based on structural complexity and explain the nutritional difference between Simple Sugars and Complex Sugars.

Model Answer:
  • Classification based on Structural Complexity:
    1. Monosaccharides: Consist of a single sugar unit that cannot be hydrolyzed into simpler carbohydrates (e.g., Glucose, Fructose, Galactose).
    2. Disaccharides: Consist of two monosaccharide units linked together (e.g., Sucrose = Glucose + Fructose; Lactose = Glucose + Galactose).
    3. Oligosaccharides: Consist of 3 to 10 monosaccharide units linked together.
    4. Polysaccharides: Consist of more than 10 monosaccharide units linked together (>10 units).
  • Nutritional Classification:
    • Simple Sugars: Include Monosaccharides and Disaccharides. They digest rapidly in the intestine, causing sharp blood glucose spikes and rapid insulin surges (e.g., pure table sugar, sweets, kunafa, basbousa, chocolate, chips).
    • Complex / Compound Sugars: Include Oligosaccharides and Polysaccharides. They are broken down slowly in the gastrointestinal tract, providing gradual glucose absorption and controlled steady insulin response, making them metabolically healthier.

6 السكريات السداسية، تحولات الأنسجة، وسوء هضم اللاكتوز Hexoses Metabolism, Tissue Conversions & Lactose Intolerance

Q14 Clinical Why Why is Glucose termed the "Blood Sugar", and what tissue-specific conversions occur in (a) Seminal Vesicles and (b) Mammary Glands?

Model Answer:
  • Why Glucose is "Blood Sugar": Glucose is the main circulating blood sugar in humans. Routine blood sugar tests specifically measure circulating glucose levels. Absorbed non-glucose monosaccharides (Fructose, Galactose, Mannose) are transported via portal circulation to the Liver, where liver enzymes convert them into Glucose before release into systemic blood.
  • Tissue-Specific Conversions:
    • (a) Seminal Vesicles / Testes: Convert blood Glucose into Fructose, which is secreted into seminal fluid to nourish spermatozoa.
    • (b) Mammary Glands: Convert blood Glucose into Galactose during lactation, combining it with Glucose to synthesize Lactose (milk sugar) in breast milk.

Q15 Pathophysiology & Management Explain the Pathophysiology of Lactose Intolerance in infants and state its dietary management.

Model Answer:
  • Pathophysiology:
    1. Caused by deficiency or absence of the intestinal Lactase enzyme.
    2. Lactase normally hydrolyzes ingested Lactose into free Glucose and Galactose for absorption.
    3. In lactase deficiency, undigested lactose remains in the intestinal lumen.
    4. Intestinal bacteria ferment undigested lactose, producing organic gases and toxic metabolites.
    5. Unabsorbed lactose exerts an osmotic effect that draws water into the intestinal lumen, causing severe abdominal colic, distension, and watery diarrhea.
  • Dietary Management:
    • Infants must not be fed natural milk.
    • They are prescribed specialized commercial LF (Lactose-Free) formulas containing pre-hydrolyzed glucose and galactose.

7 التركيب الدائري ومساقط هاوورث وشكل الكرسي Cyclic Structure, Cyclization & Projections

Q17 Chemical Evidence & Mechanism Describe the chemical evidence proving cyclic structure formation in Glucose, and detail the two-step cyclization mechanism.

Model Answer:
  • Chemical Evidence:
    1. Glucose fails to react with Schiff's reagent.
    2. Glucose fails to form a bisulfite adduct with Sodium Hydrogen Sulfite (NaHSO3).
    • This proves that the aldehyde group (−CHO) at C1 is not free, but involved in a cyclic ring structure.
  • Two-Step Cyclization Mechanism:
    1. Step 1: Hydration: Water adds across the carbonyl double bond (C=O) at C1 to form an unstable Aldenol (geminal diol) intermediate.
    2. Step 2: Intramolecular Condensation: Elimination of water between C1 and −OH on C4 or C5 of the same molecule:
      • C1 to C4 Linkage: Yields a 5-membered Furanose ring (Hemiacetal bond).
      • C1 to C5 Linkage: Yields a 6-membered Pyranose ring (Hemiacetal bond).
  • Fructose Cyclization: C2 to C5 forms Fructofuranose (5-membered); C2 to C6 forms Fructopyranose (6-membered) via Hemiketal bonds.

Q21 Short Written Define Anomeric Carbon, Specific Rotation (α), Racemic Mixture, and Resolution.

Model Answer:
  • Anomeric Carbon: The former carbonyl carbon (C1 in aldoses, C2 in ketoses) converted from achiral/symmetric to a new chiral/asymmetric carbon during ring cyclization.
  • Specific Rotation (α): The angle of optical rotation measured under standard conditions: 100g solute in 100mL solvent, tube length 10 cm (1 decimeter).
    • Glucose: Dextrorotatory (d/+), specific rotation = +52.5° (named Dextrose).
    • Fructose: Levorotatory (l/−), specific rotation = −91° (named Levulose).
  • Racemic Mixture: An equimolar mixture containing equal amounts of d(+) and l(−) isomers of a substance. It is optically inactive (0° net rotation) because equal and opposite rotations cancel each other out (+52.5° + −52.5° = 0°).
  • Resolution: The chemical or physical separation process of a racemic mixture into pure d and l optical isomers, restoring optical activity to each fraction.

Q1 Calculations & Problem Solving⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ EXAM FAVORITE Explain the 2n Stereoisomer Rule and calculate the number of stereoisomers for Glyceraldehyde, Dihydroxyacetone, Erythrose, Arabinose, and Glucose. How many Enantiomers does Glucose have?

Model Answer:
  • The 2n Rule: The total number of stereoisomers for any carbohydrate molecule is calculated using the formula 2n, where n represents the number of chiral (asymmetric) carbon atoms in that molecule.
  • Chiral Carbon Definition: A carbon atom bonded to four completely different atoms or functional groups.

Step-by-Step Calculations:

  1. Dihydroxyacetone (C3H6O3): Carbon-1 and Carbon-3 each hold two identical hydrogen atoms, while Carbon-2 forms a double bond with oxygen; therefore, it contains 0 chiral carbons (n=0).
    Number of Stereoisomers = 20 = 1 structural form  (0 optical stereoisomers)
  2. Glyceraldehyde (C3H6O3): Contains 1 chiral carbon at Carbon-2 (n=1).
    Number of Stereoisomers = 21 = 2 stereoisomers  (D-glyceraldehyde and L-glyceraldehyde)
  3. Erythrose / Erythrulose (C4H8O4): Contains 2 chiral carbons (n=2).
    Number of Stereoisomers = 22 = 4 stereoisomers
  4. Arabinose / Ribose (C5H10O5): Contains 3 chiral carbons (n=3).
    Number of Stereoisomers = 23 = 8 stereoisomers
  5. Glucose (C6H12O6 open-chain): Contains 4 chiral carbons at C2, C3, C4, and C5 (n=4).
    Number of Stereoisomers = 24 = 16 stereoisomers
  • Glucose Enantiomers Calculation (Tricky Exam Detail): Out of the 16 total stereoisomers of Glucose, ONLY 2 are true Enantiomers of each other (D-Glucose and L-Glucose). The remaining 14 stereoisomers are diastereomers (such as D-Galactose, D-Mannose, etc.).

Q2 Terminology & Distinguishing Notations Differentiate between Capital D/L Structural Configurations vs. Small d/l Optical Activities.

Model Answer:
  • Small d/l or (+)/(−) (Optical Activity):
    • Indicates the physical direction in which a compound rotates plane-polarized light in a polarimeter.
    • Small d or (+) (Dextrorotatory): Rotates plane-polarized light to the Right.
    • Small l or (−) (Levorotatory): Rotates plane-polarized light to the Left.
  • Capital D/L (Structural Spatial Configuration):
    • Represents the spatial arrangement of the hydroxyl (−OH) group on the chiral carbon furthest from the carbonyl carbon (the penultimate carbon right before the terminal −CH2OH).
    • Capital D: Hydroxyl (−OH) group is on the Right side.
    • Capital L: Hydroxyl (−OH) group is on the Left side.
  • Independence of Properties: D/L configuration and d/l optical activity are completely independent. A D-configuration sugar can be levorotatory (l), and an L-configuration sugar can be dextrorotatory (d).
  • Reference Sugar & Natural Dominance: Glyceraldehyde is the universal Reference Sugar for stereoisomerism. The vast majority of naturally occurring carbohydrates in the human body exist in the D-configuration.

Q4 Tricky Exam Detail & Proof⭐ EXAM FAVORITE Are D-Galactose and D-Mannose epimers of each other? Explain why or why not.

Model Answer:
  • Answer: NO, D-Galactose and D-Mannose are NOT epimers of each other.
  • Detailed Explanation:
    • By definition, Epimers must differ in the configuration of the hydroxyl (−OH) group at ONLY ONE single chiral carbon atom.
    • Comparing D-Galactose and D-Mannose to D-Glucose:
      • D-Mannose differs from D-Glucose at C2 ONLY (making it the C2-epimer of Glucose).
      • D-Galactose differs from D-Glucose at C4 ONLY (making it the C4-epimer of Glucose).
    • When comparing D-Galactose directly to D-Mannose, they differ in −OH configuration at TWO chiral carbons (C2 AND C4) simultaneously.
    • Therefore, D-Galactose and D-Mannose are standard diastereomers, but they cannot be classified as epimers of each other.

    • Aldaric / Saccharic Acid: Oxidation of BOTH C1 aldehyde and C6 primary alcohol groups → e.g., Glucose yields Glucaric Acid.
  2. Sugar Alcohols (Reduction Products):
    • Reduction of Glucose → Sorbitol (Glucitol).
    • Reduction of Mannose → Mannitol.
    • Reduction of Fructose → Yields a mixture of TWO sugar alcohols: Sorbitol AND Mannitol (because reducing the C2 keto group creates a new chiral center).
  3. Deoxy Sugars:
    • Formed by replacing a hydroxyl group with a hydrogen atom (removal of oxygen).
    • Example 1: 2-Deoxyribose (essential sugar component of DNA).
    • Example 2 (Exam Favorite): L-Fucose (6-deoxy-L-galactose, formed by removing oxygen at C6 of L-galactose).
  4. Amino Sugars:
    • Formed by replacing the −OH group at C2 with an amino group (−NH2).
    • Examples: Glucosamine, Galactosamine, and Mannosamine.
  5. Amino Sugar Acids (Sialic Acids):
    • Condensation of Mannosamine + Pyruvic acid → Neuraminic Acid.
    • N-acetylation of Neuraminic acid → N-acetylneuraminic acid (NANA / Sialic Acid).

Q7 Chemical Classification Define the Glycosidic Bond, state its 4 structural types, and specify the exact linkage conditions required for sugar formation.

Model Answer:
  • Definition: A covalent bond formed between the anomeric hydroxyl group (−OH) of a carbohydrate and another group (carbohydrate or non-carbohydrate).
  • Essential Linkage Condition: The bond MUST involve the anomeric −OH group at C1 in Aldoses or C2 in Ketoses.
  • Four Types of Glycosidic Bonds:
    1. O-glycosidic Bond: Linkage between anomeric −OH and an −OH group of another sugar or alcohol (standard sugar-to-sugar bond).
    2. N-glycosidic Bond: Linkage between anomeric −OH and an Amine Nitrogen (found in Nucleosides and Nucleotides binding sugar to nitrogenous bases).
    3. S-glycosidic Bond: Linkage between anomeric −OH and a Sulfhydryl (−SH) group of sulfur-containing amino acids in proteins.
    4. C-glycosidic Bond: Direct Carbon-to-Carbon bond; extremely strong and resistant to chemical cleavage.

Q10 Concept & Mechanism Explain the Concept of "Invert Sugar" and why sucrose hydrolysis inverts the direction of optical rotation.

Model Answer:
  • Pure Sucrose Optical Rotation: Pure Sucrose solution is Dextrorotatory (d/+), rotating plane-polarized light to the right with a specific rotation of +66.5°.
  • Hydrolysis Mechanism: Upon enzymatic or acid hydrolysis, the α1→β2 glycosidic bond is cleaved, releasing equimolar free D-Glucose and free D-Fructose.
    • Free D-Glucose is Dextrorotatory with a rotation of +52.5°.
    • Free D-Fructose is strongly Levorotatory with a rotation of −91°.
  • Net Calculation:
    Net Rotation = (+52.5°) + (−91°) = −38.5°  (Levorotatory)
  • Inversion Phenomenon: Because the levorotatory power of free Fructose (−91°) exceeds the dextrorotatory power of free Glucose (+52.5°), the overall net optical rotation of the solution inverts from Right (+) to Left (−); hence the hydrolyzed mixture is termed Invert Sugar.
  • Natural Example: Pure Natural Honey is composed naturally of unbonded free glucose and fructose (Invert Sugar).

Q4 Master Breakdown Classify Aliphatic Amino Acids based on Charge and Functional Groups, giving structural examples and key features for each group.

Aliphatic amino acids lack an aromatic ring and are classified into Aliphatic Neutral, Acidic, and Basic:

A. Aliphatic Neutral Amino Acids (Monoamino Monocarboxylic):

  1. Simple Aliphatic:
    • Glycine: Side chain is −H (simplest achiral amino acid).
    • Alanine: Side chain is a methyl group (−CH3).
  2. Hydroxy-containing Aliphatic:
    • Serine: Contains a primary alcohol side chain (−CH2OH).
    • Threonine: Contains a secondary alcohol side chain (−CH(OH)CH3).

  3. Branched-Chain Aliphatic ("The 3 Brothers"):
    • Valine, Leucine, and Isoleucine. Contain branched hydrocarbon side chains and display hydrophobic properties.
  4. Sulfur-containing Aliphatic:

    • Asparagine and Glutamine.
    • Why are they neutral despite having an extra nitrogen? They contain an Amide group (−CONH2). Electron resonance between the carbonyl oxygen and nitrogen prevents the amide nitrogen from accepting a proton (no basic power), so they remain strictly neutral.
  6. Imino Acid / Atypical Aliphatic:
    • Proline: Contains a secondary imino group (−NH−) incorporated into a 5-membered pyrrolidine ring. Often termed an atypical amino acid or imino acid.

B. Aliphatic Acidic Amino Acids (Monoamino Dicarboxylic Acids):

  • Contain an extra carboxyl group (−COOH) in the side chain.
  • Carry a net negative charge at physiological pH.
  • Examples: Aspartic Acid (contains −CH2COOH) and Glutamic Acid (contains −CH2CH2COOH).

C. Aliphatic Basic Amino Acids (Diamino Monocarboxylic Acids):

  • Contain an extra basic amino group in the side chain.
  • Carry a net positive charge at physiological pH.
  • Examples:
    • Lysine: Contains an ε-amino group attached to an aliphatic chain.
    • Arginine: Contains a guanidino group.
    • Histidine: Contains a basic imidazole ring. (Note: Histidine is classified as a heterocyclic basic amino acid).

Q9 Physical Properties & Zwitterion Define Optical Activity in amino acids, Zwitterion, Amphoteric Property, and Isoelectric Point (pI).

Model Answer:
  • Optical Activity: Standard amino acids contain an asymmetric α-carbon and exist as optical stereoisomers (D- and L-enantiomers).
    • L-Amino Acids: The amino group is located on the Left side; dominant form present in human body proteins.
    • D-Amino Acids: The amino group is located on the Right side; found in bacterial cell walls.
    • Glycine has no optical activity because it lacks an asymmetric carbon.
  • Melting Point: High (> 200°C) because amino acids exist in crystalline lattice forms bound by ionic and hydrogen bonds.
  • Zwitterion (Dipolar Ion): At physiological pH, an amino acid exists as a neutral dipolar ion where the α-carboxyl group loses a proton (−COO−) and the α-amino group gains a proton (−NH3+), resulting in a net electrical charge of ZERO.
  • Amphoteric Property: Ability of amino acids to act as either an acid (proton donor in alkaline medium) or a base (proton acceptor in acidic medium).
  • Isoelectric Point (pI / Isoelectric pH): The exact pH value at which an amino acid carries no net electrical charge (zwitterion form) and does NOT migrate toward either the Anode (+) or Cathode (−) in an electric field.

Q10 Calculations & Problem Solving⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ EXAM FAVORITE State the exact mathematical formulas for calculating the Isoelectric Point (pI) of Neutral, Acidic, and Basic amino acids. Solve the following 3 exam problems step-by-step:

Model Answer:

A. Formula Rules for pI Calculation:

  1. Neutral Amino Acids:
    pI = pKa1 (α-COOH) + pKa2 (α-NH3+)2
  2. Acidic Amino Acids (Dicarboxylic):
    pI = pKa1 (α-COOH) + pKaR (Side-chain −COOH)2
    (Average of the two acidic carboxyl pKa values).
  3. Basic Amino Acids (Diamino):
    pI = pKa2 (α-NH3+) + pKaR (Side-chain Amino)2
    (Average of the two basic amino pKa values).

B. Step-by-Step Problem Solutions:

Problem 1 (Neutral Amino Acid - Glycine):

Given: pKa1 (α-COOH) = 2.34, pKa2 (α-NH3+) = 9.60.

  • Classification: Neutral (Monoamino monocarboxylic).
  • Calculation:
    pI = 2.34 + 9.602 = 11.942 = 5.97

Problem 2 (Acidic Amino Acid - Aspartic Acid):

Given: pKa1 (α-COOH) = 1.99, pKa2 (α-NH3+) = 9.90, pKaR (side-chain −COOH) = 3.90.

  • Classification: Acidic (Dicarboxylic).
  • Calculation: Select the TWO acidic carboxyl pKa values (1.99 and 3.90) and ignore the amino pKa (9.90):
    pI = 1.99 + 3.902 = 5.892 = 2.945 ≈ 2.95

Problem 3 (Basic Amino Acid - Lysine / Arginine):

Given: pKa1 (α-COOH) = 2.19, pKa2 (α-NH3+) = 8.85, pKaR (side-chain amino) = 10.50.

  • Classification: Basic (Diamino).
  • Calculation: Select the TWO basic amino pKa values (8.85 and 10.50) and ignore the carboxyl pKa (2.19):
    pI = 8.85 + 10.502 = 19.352 = 9.675 ≈ 9.68

Q11 Chemical Reactions & Ionization List the chemical reactions of amino acids based on their functional groups and explain their behavior in acidic vs. alkaline media.

Model Answer:
  • Reactions due to Carboxyl Group (−COOH):
    1. Salt Formation: Reacts with alkalis to form salts.
    2. Decarboxylation: Removal of CO2 to yield primary amines (e.g., Histidine decarboxylation yields Histamine).
    3. Esterification: Reacts with alcohols to form esters.
  • Reactions due to Amino Group (−NH2):
    1. Deamination: Removal of the amino group as ammonia (NH3).
    2. Salt Formation: Reacts with acids to form amine salts.
  • Behavior in Low pH (Acidic Medium):
    • Excess H+ forces the amino acid to act as a proton acceptor.
    • Carboxylate takes a proton (−COO− + H+ → −COOH), while the amino group stays protonated (−NH3+).
    • Net Charge: POSITIVE (+) cation; migrates toward Cathode (−) in an electric field.
  • Behavior in High pH (Alkaline Medium):
    • Low H+ causes the amino acid to act as a proton donor.
    • Amino group loses a proton (−NH3+ → −NH2 + H+), while carboxyl remains ionized (−COO−).
    • Net Charge: NEGATIVE (−) anion; migrates toward Anode (+) in an electric field.
