Q2Direct Essay⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ EXAM FAVORITEWorth 3.5 Marks

Mention the Medical Importance of Pentoses in detail.

Model Answer

The medical importance of pentoses comprises 7 specific points (each worth 0.5 marks):

  1. Nucleic Acid Backbone: Ribose and Deoxyribose form the structural sugar backbone of RNA and DNA, which are essential for cellular life.
  2. Energy Currencies: Ribose enters the structure of high-energy nucleoside triphosphates including ATP and GTP (Adenosine is composed of Adenine + Ribose).
  3. Coenzymes Structure: Ribose enters the structural composition of key metabolic coenzymes such as NAD+, NADP+, and FAD.
  4. Cell Membrane Glycoproteins: Arabinose and Xylose attach to proteins to form membrane glycoproteins in the cell membrane.
  5. Heart Muscle Component: Lyxose is a specialized aldopentose present specifically in human cardiac muscle tissue.
  6. Pentose Phosphate Pathway (HMP Shunt) Intermediates: Ribose-5-phosphate and Ribulose-5-phosphate serve as vital intermediates in the Hexose Monophosphate (HMP) shunt.
  7. Uronic Acid Pathway Intermediate: Xylulose (a ketopentose) serves as an intermediate in the Uronic Acid pathway.
Q3Scientific Definition & Chemical Derivation

Define Carbohydrates scientifically and explain how the smallest functional carbohydrates are derived from glycerol.

Model Answer
  • Scientific Definition: Carbohydrates are defined as Aldehyde or Ketone derivatives of Polyhydric Alcohols, or substances that yield them upon hydrolysis.
  • Polyhydric Alcohol Definition: An alcohol containing 3 or more hydroxyl (−OH) groups.
  • Smallest Polyhydric Alcohol: Glycerol, which contains 3 carbon atoms and 3 hydroxyl groups (3 C + 3 −OH).
  • Derivation of Smallest Carbohydrates (Trioses):
    1. Oxidation of the primary alcohol group of glycerol yields Glyceraldehyde (the smallest aldotriose carbohydrate).
    2. Oxidation of the secondary alcohol group of glycerol yields Dihydroxyacetone (the smallest ketotriose carbohydrate).
    3. Their phosphorylated forms (Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate and Dihydroxyacetone-phosphate) are also classified as valid carbohydrates derived from glycerol.
Q4Chemical Evidence

Describe the chemical evidence proving that Glucose in aqueous solution exists as a cyclic ring structure rather than a open chain.

Model Answer
  • Open-chain Expectation: If Glucose existed purely as a linear open chain with a free aldehyde group (−CHO) at C1, it should undergo typical aldehyde chemical reactions.
  • Experimental Failure:
    1. Glucose fails to react with Schiff's reagent (unlike free aldehydes such as formaldehyde or acetaldehyde).
    2. Glucose fails to form a bisulfite adduct with Sodium Hydrogen Sulfite (NaHSO3).
  • Conclusion: The failure to give these typical aldehyde reactions proves that the aldehyde group at C1 is not free in solution, but is involved in an intramolecular combination forming a Cyclic Ring Structure.
Q5Cyclization Mechanism

Explain the two-step chemical mechanism of ring closure (cyclization) in Aldoses.

Model Answer
  1. Step 1: Hydration (Water Addition):
    • When an open-chain aldose is dissolved in water, a water molecule adds across the carbonyl double bond (C=O) at C1.
    • This produces an unstable intermediate containing two hydroxyl groups on C1, termed an Aldenol (geminal diol).
  2. Step 2: Intramolecular Condensation (Water Removal):
    • A water molecule is eliminated internally between C1 and a hydroxyl group on C4 or C5 of the same molecule.
    • C1 to C4 Linkage: Eliminating water between C1 and C4 forms a 5-membered ring termed Furanose (resembling a Furan ring).
    • C1 to C5 Linkage: Eliminating water between C1 and C5 forms a 6-membered ring termed Pyranose (resembling a Pyran ring).
    • The resulting bond between an aldehyde and an alcohol group is called a Hemiacetal link.
Q6Specific Rotation & Conditions

Define Specific Rotation (α) and state the exact experimental conditions and specific rotation values for Glucose and Fructose.

Model Answer
  • Definition: Specific Rotation is the exact angle of optical rotation produced by an optically active substance measured under standardized experimental conditions.
  • Standardized Experimental Conditions:
    1. Concentration: 100 grams of solute dissolved in 100 mL of solvent (water).
    2. Path Length: Sample tube length of 10 cm (1 decimeter) in a polarimeter.
  • Values:
    • Glucose: Rotates plane-polarized light to the Right (Dextrorotatory, +/d) with a specific rotation of +52.5° (hence commercially named Dextrose).
    • Fructose: Rotates plane-polarized light to the Left (Levorotatory, −/l) with a specific rotation of −91° (hence commercially named Levulose).

Q12Clinical Why

Why is Glucose termed the "Blood Sugar", and what happens metabolically to absorbed Fructose and Galactose in the liver?

Model Answer
  • Why Glucose is "Blood Sugar": Glucose is the only monosaccharide that circulates freely in human blood to supply energy to body tissues. Routine clinical blood sugar tests specifically measure circulating glucose levels.
  • Metabolic Fate of Fructose and Galactose:
    1. All non-glucose dietary monosaccharides (Fructose, Galactose, Mannose) absorbed from the intestine travel via the portal vein to the Liver.
    2. The liver possesses specialized enzymes that convert all absorbed Fructose and Galactose into Glucose before releasing them into the systemic circulation.
Q13Clinical Why

Explain the tissue-specific conversions of Glucose in (a) Seminal Vesicles and (b) Mammary Glands.

Model Answer
  • (a) Seminal Vesicles:
    • Seminal vesicles uptake circulating blood Glucose and convert it into Fructose.
    • Fructose is secreted into seminal fluid as the primary energy substrate to nourish and power spermatozoa movement.
  • (b) Mammary Glands:
    • During lactation, mammary glands uptake blood Glucose and convert a portion of it into Galactose.
    • Galactose is then condensed with Glucose to synthesize Lactose (milk sugar) in breast milk.
Q14Pathophysiology & Management

Explain the Pathophysiology of Lactose Intolerance in infants and state its dietary management.

Model Answer
  • Pathophysiology:
    1. Caused by a genetic deficiency or absence of the intestinal enzyme Lactase.
    2. Normally, Lactase hydrolyzes ingested Lactose into free Glucose and Galactose for intestinal absorption.
    3. In lactase deficiency, undigested lactose remains in the intestinal lumen.
    4. Intestinal bacteria ferment undigested lactose, producing organic gases and toxic metabolites.
    5. Unabsorbed lactose exerts an osmotic effect that draws water into the intestinal lumen, causing severe abdominal colic, distension, and watery diarrhea.
  • Dietary Management:
    • Infants must not be fed natural milk.
    • They are prescribed specialized commercial LF (Lactose-Free) formulas where lactose is replaced with pre-hydrolyzed glucose or fructose.

Q15Formula & Calculations

Explain the 2n Stereoisomer Rule and calculate the number of stereoisomers for Glyceraldehyde, Dihydroxyacetone, Erythrose, Ribose, and Glucose.

Model Answer
  • Formula: Number of Stereoisomers = 2n, where n is the number of chiral (asymmetric) carbon atoms in the molecule.
  • Chiral Carbon Definition: A carbon atom bonded to 4 completely different atoms or groups.
1. Dihydroxyacetone: 0 chiral carbons (n=0) → 2⁰ = 1 structural form (0 optical stereoisomers). 2. Glyceraldehyde: 1 chiral carbon (n=1) → 2¹ = 2 stereoisomers (D- and L-glyceraldehyde). 3. Erythrose: 2 chiral carbons (n=2) → 2² = 4 stereoisomers. 4. Ribose: 3 chiral carbons (n=3) → 2³ = 8 stereoisomers. 5. Glucose (Open-chain): 4 chiral carbons at C2, C3, C4, C5 (n=4) → 2⁴ = 16 stereoisomers. 6. Fructose (Open-chain): 3 chiral carbons at C3, C4, C5 (n=3) → 2³ = 8 stereoisomers.
Q16Anomeric Carbon Definition

What is the Anomeric Carbon, how does it originate during cyclization, and where is it located in Aldoses vs. Ketoses?

Model Answer
  • Definition: The Anomeric Carbon is the former carbonyl carbon atom that is converted from an achiral/symmetric carbon into a new chiral/asymmetric carbon atom upon ring cyclization.
  • Origin: In open-chain form, C1 of aldoses is symmetric (C=O double bond). Upon hydration and intramolecular ring closure, C1 becomes bonded to 4 different groups (−OH, −H, −O-ring, and C2 chain), creating a new chiral center.
  • Location:
    • Located at Carbon-1 (C1) in Aldoses.
    • Located at Carbon-2 (C2) in Ketoses.

Q1Calculations & Problem Solving⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ EXAM FAVORITE

Explain the 2n Stereoisomer Rule and calculate the number of stereoisomers for Glyceraldehyde, Dihydroxyacetone, Erythrose, Arabinose, and Glucose. How many Enantiomers does Glucose have?

Model Answer
  • The 2n Rule: The total number of stereoisomers for any carbohydrate molecule is calculated using the formula 2n, where n represents the number of chiral (asymmetric) carbon atoms in that molecule.
  • Chiral Carbon Definition: A carbon atom bonded to four completely different atoms or functional groups.
1. Dihydroxyacetone (C₃H₆O₃): 0 chiral carbons (n=0). Stereoisomers = 2⁰ = 1 structural form (0 optical stereoisomers). 2. Glyceraldehyde (C₃H₆O₃): 1 chiral carbon at C2 (n=1). Stereoisomers = 2¹ = 2 stereoisomers (D-glyceraldehyde and L-glyceraldehyde). 3. Erythrose (C₄H₈O₄): 2 chiral carbons (n=2). Stereoisomers = 2² = 4 stereoisomers. 4. Arabinose / Ribose (C₅H₁₀O₅): 3 chiral carbons (n=3). Stereoisomers = 2³ = 8 stereoisomers. 5. Glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆ open-chain): 4 chiral carbons at C2, C3, C4, C5 (n=4). Stereoisomers = 2⁴ = 16 stereoisomers.

Glucose Enantiomers Calculation (Tricky Exam Detail): Out of the 16 total stereoisomers of Glucose, ONLY 2 are true Enantiomers of each other (D-Glucose and L-Glucose). The remaining 14 stereoisomers are diastereomers (such as D-Galactose, D-Mannose, etc.).

Q2Terminology & Distinguishing Notations

Differentiate between Capital D/L Structural Configurations vs. Small d/l Optical Activities.

Model Answer
  • Small d/l or (+)/(−) (Optical Activity):
    • Indicates the physical direction in which a compound rotates plane-polarized light in a polarimeter.
    • Small d or (+) (Dextrorotatory): Rotates plane-polarized light to the Right.
    • Small l or (−) (Levorotatory): Rotates plane-polarized light to the Left.
  • Capital D/L (Structural Spatial Configuration):
    • Represents the spatial arrangement of the hydroxyl (−OH) group on the chiral carbon furthest from the carbonyl carbon (the penultimate carbon right before the terminal −CH2OH).
    • Capital D: Hydroxyl (−OH) group is on the Right side.
    • Capital L: Hydroxyl (−OH) group is on the Left side.
  • Independence of Properties: D/L configuration and d/l optical activity are completely independent. A D-configuration sugar can be levorotatory (l), and an L-configuration sugar can be dextrorotatory (d).
  • Reference Sugar & Natural Dominance: Glyceraldehyde is the universal Reference Sugar for stereoisomerism. The vast majority of naturally occurring carbohydrates in the human body exist in the D-configuration.

Q4Tricky Exam Detail & Proof

Are D-Galactose and D-Mannose epimers of each other? Explain why or why not.

Model Answer

Answer: NO, D-Galactose and D-Mannose are NOT epimers of each other.

  • By definition, Epimers must differ in the configuration of the hydroxyl (−OH) group at ONLY ONE single chiral carbon atom.
  • Comparing D-Galactose and D-Mannose to D-Glucose:
    • D-Mannose differs from D-Glucose at C₂ ONLY (making it the C₂-epimer of Glucose).
    • D-Galactose differs from D-Glucose at C₄ ONLY (making it the C₄-epimer of Glucose).
  • When comparing D-Galactose directly to D-Mannose, they differ in −OH configuration at TWO chiral carbons (C₂ AND C₄) simultaneously.
  • Therefore, D-Galactose and D-Mannose are standard diastereomers, but they cannot be classified as epimers of each other.

Q5Direct Essay & Categorization

List the 5 major classes of Monosaccharide Derivatives, giving chemical reactions and examples for each.

Model Answer
  1. Sugar Acids (Oxidation Products):
    • Aldonic Acid: Oxidation of the C₁ aldehyde group → e.g., Glucose yields Gluconic Acid.
    • Uronic Acid: Oxidation of the C₆ primary alcohol group → e.g., Glucose yields Glucuronic Acid.
    • Aldaric / Saccharic Acid: Oxidation of BOTH C₁ aldehyde and C₆ primary alcohol groups → e.g., Glucose yields Glucaric Acid.
  2. Sugar Alcohols (Reduction Products):
    • Reduction of Glucose → Sorbitol (Glucitol).
    • Reduction of Mannose → Mannitol.
    • Reduction of Fructose → Yields a mixture of TWO sugar alcohols: Sorbitol AND Mannitol (because reducing the C₂ keto group creates a new chiral center).
  3. Deoxy Sugars:
    • Formed by replacing a hydroxyl group with a hydrogen atom (removal of oxygen).
    • Example 1: 2-Deoxyribose (essential sugar component of DNA).
    • Example 2 (Exam Favorite): L-Fucose (6-deoxy-L-galactose, formed by removing oxygen at C₆ of L-galactose).
  4. Amino Sugars:
    • Formed by replacing the −OH group at C₂ with an amino group (−NH₂).
    • Examples: Glucosamine, Galactosamine, and Mannosamine.
  5. Amino Sugar Acids (Sialic Acids):
    • Condensation of Mannosamine + Pyruvic acid → Neuraminic Acid.
    • N-acetylation of Neuraminic acid → N-acetylneuraminic acid (NANA / Sialic Acid).
Q6Clinical Why⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ EXAM FAVORITE

Explain the Clinical Pharmacy application of Mannitol Solution as an Osmotic Diuretic.

Model Answer
  • Mechanism of Action: Mannitol is a sugar alcohol that acts as an Osmotic Diuretic. It remains in the vascular/renal compartment and exerts an osmotic pull that draws excess fluid and water out of body tissues into the bloodstream to be excreted in urine.
  • Clinical Indications:
    1. Increased Intracranial Pressure (ICP): Administered intravenously to brain surgery patients or trauma cases to withdraw cerebral edema fluid from brain tissue, preventing fatal brain compression.
    2. Increased Intraocular Pressure (Glaucoma): Used to reduce severe fluid pressure inside the eyeball.

Q7Chemical Classification

Define the Glycosidic Bond, state its 4 structural types, and specify the exact linkage conditions required for sugar formation.

Model Answer
  • Definition: A covalent bond formed between the anomeric hydroxyl group (−OH) of a carbohydrate and another group (carbohydrate or non-carbohydrate).
  • Essential Linkage Condition: The bond MUST involve the anomeric −OH group at C₁ in Aldoses or C₂ in Ketoses.
  • Four Types of Glycosidic Bonds:
    1. O-glycosidic Bond: Linkage between anomeric −OH and an −OH group of another sugar or alcohol (standard sugar-to-sugar bond).
    2. N-glycosidic Bond: Linkage between anomeric −OH and an Amine Nitrogen (found in Nucleosides and Nucleotides binding sugar to nitrogenous bases).
    3. S-glycosidic Bond: Linkage between anomeric −OH and a Sulfhydryl (−SH) group of sulfur-containing amino acids in proteins.
    4. C-glycosidic Bond: Direct Carbon-to-Carbon bond; extremely strong and resistant to chemical cleavage.

Q9Clinical Why & Pathophysiology

Explain the Pathophysiology of Lactose Intolerance and its clinical dietary management in infants.

Model Answer
  • Pathophysiology:
    1. Caused by a genetic or acquired deficiency of the brush-border intestinal enzyme Lactase.
    2. In healthy infants, Lactase hydrolyzes ingested Lactose into free Glucose and Galactose for intestinal absorption.
    3. In lactase deficiency, undigested lactose remains in the intestinal lumen.
    4. Intestinal bacteria ferment undigested lactose, producing organic acids and hydrogen gas.
    5. Unabsorbed lactose exerts an osmotic effect that draws water into the lumen, causing flatulence, severe abdominal cramps, distension, and watery diarrhea.
  • Dietary Management:
    • Infant natural breastfeeding or standard milk must be stopped.
    • The infant is fed specialized commercial LF (Lactose-Free) infant formulas containing pre-hydrolyzed glucose and galactose.
Q10Concept & Mechanism

Explain the Concept of "Invert Sugar" and why sucrose hydrolysis inverts the direction of optical rotation.

Model Answer
  • Pure Sucrose Optical Rotation: Pure Sucrose solution is Dextrorotatory (d/+), rotating plane-polarized light to the right with a specific rotation of +66.5°.
  • Hydrolysis Mechanism: Upon enzymatic or acid hydrolysis, the α1→β2 glycosidic bond is cleaved, releasing equimolar free D-Glucose and free D-Fructose.
    • Free D-Glucose is Dextrorotatory with a rotation of +52.5°.
    • Free D-Fructose is strongly Levorotatory with a rotation of −91°.
Net Rotation = (+52.5°) + (−91°) = −38.5° (Levorotatory)
  • Inversion Phenomenon: Because the levorotatory power of free Fructose (−91°) exceeds the dextrorotatory power of free Glucose (+52.5°), the overall net optical rotation of the solution inverts from Right (+) to Left (−); hence the hydrolyzed mixture is termed Invert Sugar.
  • Natural Example: Pure Natural Honey is composed naturally of unbonded free glucose and fructose (Invert Sugar).

Q12Physiological Contrast

Contrast the Metabolic Roles of Liver Glycogen vs. Muscle Glycogen.

Model Answer
  • Liver Glycogen ("Unselfish / Generous"):
    • Synthesized and stored in the liver to maintain systemic blood glucose homeostasis during fasting.

Q14Resilience Mechanism & Master GAG Table

Explain the "Sponge / Resilience Mechanism" of Glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) and summarize the 5 GAGs with their unique features.

Model Answer

Sponge / Resilience Mechanism:

  • GAGs consist of long, unbranched repeating disaccharide chains containing high concentrations of negative charges (−COO⁻ and −SO₄²⁻).

  • Under joint compression (e.g., jumping), water is squeezed out to cushion the force; when compression is released, water immediately re-enters, providing mechanical elasticity and shock absorption in joints.

Q2Structure & Exception

Describe the general chemical structure of an amino acid. Identify the central carbon atom and name its 4 attached groups. State the exception to chiral asymmetry.

Model Answer

Q3Master Breakdown

Classify Aliphatic Amino Acids based on Charge and Functional Groups, giving structural examples for each category.

Model Answer

Aliphatic amino acids lack an aromatic ring and are categorized into Aliphatic Neutral, Acidic, and Basic:

A. Aliphatic Neutral Amino Acids (Monoamino Monocarboxylic Acids):

  1. Simple Aliphatic:
    • Glycine: Side chain is −H (smallest/simplest amino acid).
    • Alanine: Side chain is a methyl group (−CH₃).
  2. Hydroxy-containing Aliphatic:
    • Serine: Contains a primary alcohol side chain (−CH₂OH).
    • Threonine: Contains a secondary alcohol side chain (−CH(OH)CH₃).
    • (Note: Hydroxyl groups affect polarity and water solubility but do NOT alter the neutral charge state).
  3. Branched-Chain Aliphatic (The 3 Brothers):
    • Valine, Leucine, and Isoleucine. They contain branched hydrocarbon chains and exhibit hydrophobic properties.
  4. Sulfur-containing Aliphatic:
    • Cysteine: Contains a sulfhydryl / thiol group (−CH₂SH).
    • Methionine: Contains a thioether group (−CH₂-CH₂-S-CH₃).
  5. Amide group-containing Aliphatic (⭐ EXAM TRICK):
    • Asparagine (derived from Aspartic acid) and Glutamine (derived from Glutamic acid).
    • Why are they neutral despite having an extra nitrogen? They contain an Amide group (−CONH₂). Resonance between the carbonyl oxygen and nitrogen prevents the amide nitrogen from accepting a proton (no basic power), so they remain strictly neutral.
  6. Imino Acid / Atypical Aliphatic:
    • Proline: Contains a secondary imino group (−NH−) incorporated into a 5-membered pyrrolidine ring. Often termed an atypical amino acid or imino acid.

B. Aliphatic Acidic Amino Acids (Monoamino Dicarboxylic Acids):

  • Contain an extra carboxyl group (−COOH) in the side chain.
  • Carry a net negative charge at physiological pH.
  • Examples: Aspartic Acid (contains −CH₂COOH) and Glutamic Acid (contains −CH₂CH₂COOH).

C. Aliphatic Basic Amino Acids (Diamino Monocarboxylic Acids):

  • Contain an extra basic amino group in the side chain.
  • Carry a net positive charge at physiological pH.
  • Examples:
    • Lysine: Contains an ε-amino group attached to an aliphatic chain.
    • Arginine: Contains a guanidino group.
    • Histidine: Contains a basic imidazole ring.

Q8Physical Properties & Zwitterion

Define Optical Activity in amino acids, Zwitterion, Amphoteric Property, and Isoelectric Point (pI).

Model Answer
  • Optical Activity: Standard amino acids contain a chiral α-carbon and exist as optical stereoisomers (D- and L-enantiomers).
    • L-Amino Acids: The amino group is located on the Left side; dominant form present in human body proteins.
    • D-Amino Acids: The amino group is located on the Right side; found exclusively in bacterial cell walls.
    • Glycine has no optical activity because it lacks an asymmetric carbon.
  • Melting Point: High (> 200°C) because amino acids exist in crystalline lattice forms bound by ionic and hydrogen bonds.
  • Zwitterion (Dipolar Ion): At physiological pH, an amino acid exists as a neutral dipolar ion where the α-carboxyl group loses a proton (−COO⁻) and the α-amino group gains a proton (−NH₃⁺), resulting in a net electrical charge of ZERO.
  • Amphoteric Property: Ability of amino acids to act as either an acid (proton donor in alkaline medium) or a base (proton acceptor in acidic medium).
  • Isoelectric Point (pI / Isoelectric pH): The exact pH value at which an amino acid carries no net electrical charge (zwitterion form) and does NOT migrate toward either the Anode (+) or Cathode (−) in an electric field.
Q9Calculations & Problem Solving⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ EXAM FAVORITE

State the exact mathematical formulas for calculating the Isoelectric Point (pI) of Neutral, Acidic, and Basic amino acids. Solve the following 3 exam problems step-by-step.

Model Answer

A. Formula Rules for pI Calculation:

1. Neutral Amino Acids:   pI = [ pKa1 (α-COOH) + pKa2 (α-NH₃⁺) ] / 2
2. Acidic Amino Acids (Dicarboxylic):   pI = [ pKa1 (α-COOH) + pKaR (Side-chain −COOH) ] / 2
(Average of the two acidic carboxyl pKa values)
3. Basic Amino Acids (Diamino):   pI = [ pKa2 (α-NH₃⁺) + pKaR (Side-chain Amino) ] / 2
(Average of the two basic amino pKa values)

B. Step-by-Step Problem Solutions:

Problem 1 (Neutral — Glycine): Given pKa1 (α-COOH)=2.34, pKa2 (α-NH₃⁺)=9.60. Classification: Neutral (Monoamino monocarboxylic). pI = (2.34 + 9.60)/2 = 11.94/2 = 5.97
Problem 2 (Acidic — Aspartic Acid): Given pKa1=1.99, pKa2=9.90, pKaR (side-chain −COOH)=3.90. Classification: Acidic (Dicarboxylic). Select the TWO acidic carboxyl pKa values (1.99 and 3.90), ignore the amino pKa (9.90): pI = (1.99 + 3.90)/2 = 5.89/2 = 2.945 ≈ 2.95
Problem 3 (Basic — Lysine/Arginine): Given pKa1=2.19, pKa2=8.85, pKaR (side-chain amino)=10.50. Classification: Basic (Diamino). Select the TWO basic amino pKa values (8.85 and 10.50), ignore the carboxyl pKa (2.19): pI = (8.85 + 10.50)/2 = 19.35/2 = 9.675 ≈ 9.68

Q10Chemical Reactions & Titration

List the chemical reactions of amino acids based on their functional groups and explain their behavior in acidic vs. alkaline media.

Model Answer
  • Reactions due to Carboxyl Group (−COOH):
    1. Salt Formation: Reacts with bases/alkalis to form salts.
    2. Decarboxylation: Removal of CO₂ to yield primary amines (e.g., Histidine → Histamine).
    3. Esterification: Reacts with alcohols to form esters.
  • Reactions due to Amino Group (−NH₂):
    1. Deamination: Removal of the amino group as ammonia (NH₃).
    2. Salt Formation: Reacts with acids to form amine salts.
  • Behavior in Low pH (Acidic Medium):
    • Excess H⁺ ions force the amino acid to act as a proton acceptor.
    • The carboxylate ion takes a proton (−COO⁻ + H⁺ → −COOH), while the amino group remains protonated (−NH₃⁺).
    • Net Charge: POSITIVE (+) cation; moves toward Cathode.
  • Behavior in High pH (Alkaline Medium):
    • Low H⁺ causes the amino acid to act as a proton donor.
    • The amino group loses a proton (−NH₃⁺ → −NH₂ + H⁺), while the carboxyl group stays ionized (−COO⁻).
    • Net Charge: NEGATIVE (−) anion; moves toward Anode.
